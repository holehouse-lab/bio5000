## Overview

DGE is an interdisciplinary journal club focused on papers that explore how intrinsically disordered regions regulate or contribute to gene expression. We will focus on papers that integrate across scales, concepts, and disciplines to understand the **molecular details** surrounding how proteins and other biomolecules dictate eukaryotic transcriptional regulation.

Relevant papers focus on topics including (but *not limited* to):

1. How transcription factors find, recognize, and discriminate between their target sites
2. TF effector domains: sequence features, molecular grammar, and mechanism
3. Coactivators, Co-repressors, and RNA polymerase II machinery
4. Chromatin organization, remodeling, and epigenetic memory
6. Condensates, hubs, and the spatial organization of transcription
8. Evolution of disordered regions and the regulatory proteins that contain them
9. High-throughput, single-molecule, and computational approaches for mapping sequence to function

## Expectations

**Everyone:** Everyone should read (at least skim...) each paper each week. However, the expectation is *not* that everyone reads every paper in detail each week. This would be great, but it is unrealistic, especially given the breadth of topics.

**Presenter:** The presenter should present, assuming *no one* has read the paper. This **does not mean no one should read the paper**, but to ensure presentations are stand-alone and straightforward, the presenter must provide all the requisite context and background. Alex (and hopefully others) will ask "dumb" questions if concepts aren't explained (this is fine and not a problem; just a disclaimer!). <mark>For the week you are presenting, you must be the MASTER of your paper. DO NOT leave preparation until the night before.</mark>

**Auditors:** Come to whichever papers interest you most!

**For credit:** If you're taking the course for credit, you're expected to come to every class (please email Alex if you can't make it). Also, please follow the format instructions below. We have up to 17 for-credit slots.

## Presentation format

Each week, one person presents a paper, providing an intuitive overview and critique of the ideas, techniques, and conclusions. The presentation should use slides (or be a full "chalk talk"), but if slides are used, they must have an (approximately) consistent format.

### Introduction

The presentation should open with three slides:

1. **WHAT**

    A single slide summarizing the core question being addressed in this paper.

2. **WHY**

    A single slide summarizing the context for why this question is worth answering.

3. **HOW**

    A single slide summarizing the approaches used to answer the question.

Many (most) papers are not written to explicitly articulate the WHAT, WHY, and HOW, but all good papers feature these three pillars, implicitly if not explicitly. These slides should not be super detailed. They should enable discussion and prime the room for the subsequent presentation.

**It should be possible for someone to leave the room after seeing JUST these three slides and understand the central message of the paper and why it matters.**

This introduction should take 5–10 minutes with questions, or 2–5 without.

### Body

Once these three intro slides have been presented, the presenter should present results from the paper to support their WHAT/WHY/HOW statements.

The presenter does NOT need to present every figure/result from the paper but should build a series of slides that walk the audience through its results to support their WHAT/WHY/HOW introduction. Exactly how you (the presenter) do this is up to you, but there are some constraints:

1. **You can include NO MORE THAN 10 data slides in the main presentation** (but you can include additional slides as backup if you want). Data slides show results; generally, you should only show one "big" result per slide.
2. **For each new technique being presented, you must EXPLAIN how it works.** This can be done with a dedicated technique slide (probably useful for complicated techniques), or you can take a second to explain the technique on the slide. These explanations do not need to be super detailed, but you should ensure your audience understands (1) WHAT the technique is measuring, (2) WHY that is a useful thing to measure, and (3) HOW, at a high level, it works. Focus on an intuitive explanation, not technical details (although we can dive into those details IF they are your interest and specialty, but this is at the presenter's discretion). Technique slides don't count as data slides.
3. **You must pick ONE assay/experiment in the paper and explain how it works.** This will require you to go into the Methods section and, likely beyond, back to other literature. Specifically:
    - What is being measured, and how is it being quantified?
    - How is that "signal" being processed and analyzed statistically/mathematically/theoretically?
    - Any caveats or limitations?
4. **At the end of the presentation, you should list three limitations of the paper.** We will discuss these (and others) as a group. The goal here is NOT to deconstruct the results or conclusions but to be realistic about the limitations and discuss collectively what these limitations mean for the interpretation of the data.

Alternatively, you are WELCOME to chalk talk the entire thing, but if you do, please keep the WHAT/WHY/HOW points in mind and explicitly address them.
